The lighthouse had been dark for three winters, and still the gulls came at dusk to circle the lantern room as if expecting it to wake. Edda watched them from the kitchen window, a cup of cooling tea between her hands, and counted the ones she recognized by the broken set of a wing or the particular insolence of their crying.

Her father had kept the light for forty years. He had taught her the trick of the wick and the patience of the long nights, and he had told her, more than once, that a keeper does not tend the lamp for the ships she can see but for the ones she cannot. She had thought it sentimental then. She was sixteen and certain the sea owed her something more interesting than weather.

Now the lamp was cold and the village had its electric beacon on the far headland, efficient and joyless, and no one needed an old woman on a rock. They had told her so kindly, which was worse. She had nodded and signed their papers and agreed to be moved in the spring.

But spring was not here yet. Tonight the fog came in low and certain, swallowing the harbor lights one by one, and somewhere out past the reef a boat's horn sounded twice, then went quiet in a way that horns should not. Edda set down her tea. She knew that silence. She had grown up inside it.

She climbed the tower stairs in the dark, her hand finding each worn dip in the rail without being told, and at the top she struck a match the way her father had shown her, cupping the flame against the draft. The wick caught. The old glass threw its long arm of light across the water, and the fog turned gold, and the horn out past the reef sounded again — three times now, alive, answered.

She stayed until morning. She did not sign their papers in the spring.
